Why Accessibility Isn't Optional

Byline: September 3, 2026 by Maya Okonkwo

Accessibility is often treated as an afterthought, something to bolt on right before launch. But building with accessibility in mind from the start costs far less than retrofitting it later, and it makes your site better for every single visitor, not just those using assistive technology.

Why It Matters

When a site is accessible, it means more people can actually use it. Screen reader users, keyboard-only users, people with low vision, and people with motor impairments all benefit from thoughtful markup.

  • Reaches a wider audience, including the estimated 1 in 4 adults living with a disability
  • Improves SEO, since accessible markup and search engine parsing overlap heavily
  • Reduces legal risk, as accessibility lawsuits have become increasingly common
  • Often improves usability for everyone, not just people with disabilities

Common Mistakes

Many teams focus on visual polish while ignoring semantic meaning. Using a div styled to look like a button is technically clickable, but it's invisible to a screen reader unless marked up correctly. Semantic HTML is the foundation accessibility is built on — no amount of ARIA patching fixes a fundamentally non-semantic structure.

Keyboard Navigation

Every interactive element should be reachable and operable using only a keyboard. Try unplugging your mouse for ten minutes and navigating your own site — it's the fastest way to find what's broken.

"The power of the Web is in its universality. Access by everyone regardless of disability is an essential aspect."

--Tim Berners-Lee, W3C

Code Sample

A properly labeled button uses the native button element instead of a styled div, which gives you keyboard support and screen reader announcements for free. 

 <button type="submit">Save changes</button>
